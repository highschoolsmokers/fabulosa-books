The store

Fabulosa Books is an independent bookstore in San Francisco's Castro. It specializes in LGBTQIA+ titles, with those sections displayed prominently at the front of the shop. I work there, and I built this scheduler for the store. This tutorial walks through the same build, one file at a time.

The staff are the real crew, by first name. Their availability in the examples is made up; the store's hours and its scheduling problem are real.

The store's history

Fabulosa Books is an independent bookstore in San Francisco's Castro that specializes in LGBTQIA+ titles. It occupies the address where A Different Light once stood.

The Castro is a historically gay neighborhood, and young gay people came to it from across the country.

Walk a block south to 575 Castro Street, where Harvey Milk ran Castro Camera from 1972 until he was assassinated in 1978. He was the first openly gay man elected to public office in California.

In 1978, while Milk was in office, 489 Castro Street was the Obelisk, a designer gift shop. I see its obelisk-shaped door handles every day.

A Different Light, a chain of LGBT bookstores, opened a branch here later in the 1980s. Thom Gunn and Kevin Killian read in its gay series. Dorothy Allison and Kitty Tsui read in its lesbian series.

The store opened its space to outside groups, including ACT UP (the AIDS Coalition To Unleash Power), HomoCore, and GLOE (Gay & Lesbian Outreach and Engagement).

HomoCore began in San Francisco in 1988 as a zine for young hardcore punks in the gay underground.

After the chain closed in 2011, Dog Eared Books moved into the space in 2016.

Armistead Maupin and Brontez Purnell read at Dog Eared's opening party that June.

In 2021, the space reopened as Fabulosa Books.

Susan Stryker presented Transgender History here in 2026, and Michelle Tea featured at a book launch.

A cat calendar on the wall

The schedule hung behind the register on a cat calendar. The manager wrote it in by hand each week.

To see a shift, you came into the store and read the wall. To trade one, you crossed out a name and put another in its place.

Writing the names in was the easy part. The manager also carried everything the calendar could not show: who couldn't work Sundays, who had asked for a Wednesday off, who held keys, who was close to the weekly limit, and who had closed three Saturdays running.

Hours and shifts

The store is open 10am–8pm Sunday through Thursday, 10am–9pm Friday, and 10am–10pm Saturday. Each day has three six-hour shifts. Closing ends when the store closes.

What you'll build

You build a web app where Alvin types "Schedule next week" and gets back a complete, legal week to review. The chat runs one scheduling agent with seven tools. The agent reads the staff and their availability, proposes a week, reads back every rule the week breaks, and fixes the week until it breaks none. A fairness reviewer checks how the closings and weekend shifts fall. Nothing saves until Alvin approves it in the chat.

The stack:

  • Next.js 16 for the app and its one API route.
  • AI SDK 7 for the agent loop, the tools, and the chat UI.
  • Vercel AI Gateway for model access, with no provider key in the code.
  • Neon for Postgres.
  • Vitest for the tests, including tests that drive the agent with a scripted model.

Every code block in this tutorial comes from the finished app. A test in the app fails if a block here stops matching the source.

What you need

  • Node.js 22 or later.
  • A Vercel account and team, for the AI Gateway and the Neon database.
  • A credit card on the Vercel team. Without one, every model call fails (see Part 3).
  • Some TypeScript and React. Parts 5 and 6 explain the agent and chat code. The other parts move faster.
